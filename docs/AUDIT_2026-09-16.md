# Pre-submission audit, 2026-09-16

Two claims were tested the way a referee would test them. One did not survive. The other
turned into a better result than the claim it replaced.

Tools: [`tools/audit_ilu_hole.py`](../tools/audit_ilu_hole.py),
[`tools/probe_rho_search.py`](../tools/probe_rho_search.py).
Data: `results/tables/ilu_hole_audit.csv`, `results/tables/rho_search_probe.csv`.

---

## 1. The ILU hole was our retry ladder — claim retracted

### What we claimed

> 166 matrices on which no incomplete factorization can be built, which takes the entire
> preconditioned family down at once. Step 1 makes 150 of the 166 constructible.

### What the audit found

`build_ilu` retried spilu at `(1e-3, 5)`, `(1e-2, 10)`, `(1e-2, 20)` — *increasing*
`drop_tol`. spilu fails on a zero pivot, and dropping more entries makes a zero pivot
likelier, not less. The ladder was retrying in the direction that makes failure more
probable. It never varied `diag_pivot_thresh` or `permc_spec` either.

Re-running all 166 against stock spilu, **with no permutation at all**:

| setting | usable factorization |
|---|---|
| `ours_1` (1e-3, 5) | 0 / 166 |
| `ours_2` (1e-2, 10) | 0 / 166 |
| `ours_3` (1e-2, 20) | 0 / 166 |
| `permc_natural` (1e-4, 20) | 14 / 166 |
| `full_pivot` (1e-3, 10, thresh 1.0) | 9 / 166 |
| `permc_mmd` (1e-4, 20, MMD_AT_PLUS_A) | 44 / 166 |
| **`low_drop` (1e-6, 20)** | **113 / 166** |
| **`no_drop` (0.0, 50)** | **150 / 166** |

150 of 166 factor without any permutation. And it is the *same* 150 — the reordering
rescue set and the `no_drop` set differ by one matrix each way (`eurqsa`, `west0156`),
149 in common.

There is no cost defence either. `no_drop` takes 0.002 s median and 23.4 s in total across
all 150. It is not buying feasibility at a price; it is simply the setting that should
have been tried.

### What survives

Restricting to a genuinely *incomplete* factorization rather than the near-complete
`no_drop`, `low_drop` at `drop_tol = 1e-6` still factors **113 of the 166**. The claim
fails on either reading.

**16 matrices resist every setting tried.** That is the real hole, and it is a tenth of
the size we reported.

### Consequences

1. `build_ilu` is fixed ([`config.ILU_LADDER`](../src/solvebench/config.py)): the ladder
   now pushes toward robustness `(1e-3,5) → (1e-4,10) → (1e-6,20) → (0,50)`, and every
   candidate is probed on a random vector before being accepted. A factorization that
   succeeds and then maps to `inf` was previously returned silently and poisoned every
   downstream Krylov step — `permc_mmd` built 45 and only 44 were usable, so the case is
   real, not hypothetical. 47 tests still pass.
2. **Every ILU number in the project is understated and must be re-measured.** The main
   sweep, the full-corpus reordering result (616 → 634), and the ILU-hole figure all rest
   on the broken ladder. This is a re-run, not an edit.
3. The refuted claim still appears in `SCOPE.md`, `RESULTS.md`, `docs/GUIDE.md`,
   `docs/PROJECT_STATE.md`, `Presentation/explaination.md` and `Presentation/present.tex`.
   Only `SCOPE.md` has been corrected so far.

## 2. Why the bottleneck objective converts only two systems

This began as a weakness to explain and ended as the sharper result.

The bottleneck objective minimises the worst row ratio, is solved exactly, and was
verified against brute force 400/400. Against MC64 it wins **365-0** with 522 ties. It
converts **+2** systems.

The reason is a threshold:

```
MC64       worst ratio < 1 :  43 matrices
bottleneck worst ratio < 1 :  43 matrices
bottleneck crosses below 1 where MC64 did not :  0
the reverse                                   :  0
```

The row-ratio bound certifies convergence below 1 and says nothing above it. Bottleneck
never once moves a matrix across that line. Every one of its 365 wins happens far above:

```
MC64 ratio 2 - 10     :  20 wins
MC64 ratio 10 - 1e3   : 175 wins
MC64 ratio > 1e3      : 170 wins

median ratio among the wins : 597 -> 368   (35.8% better)
median rho(T_GS) there      : 117.8
```

A 36% improvement to a quantity whose value is 597, on matrices whose Gauss-Seidel
iteration grows by a factor of 118 per step. The objective is continuous; the bound it
feeds is a step function at 1; real matrices sit orders of magnitude from the step.

**The bound's reach, measured.** Of 125 matrices with rho(T_GS) < 1, only 23 are strictly
diagonally dominant — 18.4%. Four convergent matrices in five converge for reasons the
row-ratio bound cannot see. Within the SDD regime the ratio predicts rho almost perfectly
(Spearman +0.988, n = 23); outside it, +0.576 with 42% converging anyway.

> Optimising a sufficient condition is worth nothing when the condition is a threshold and
> the data is nowhere near it. The bottleneck objective is an exact solution to the wrong
> problem, and the 365-0 record is what being exactly right about the wrong quantity looks
> like.

That is a result, not an excuse, and it generalises past this project.

## 3. What follows: target rho, not a bound on rho

If the bound is the problem, target what decides. rho(T_GS) is not an assignment problem
and cannot be minimised exactly, but it can be searched — start from the portfolio's
choice and swap row pairs while a power-iteration estimate falls
([`tools/probe_rho_search.py`](../tools/probe_rho_search.py)).

Permutation does move rho: across the 262 matrices with a finite estimate under both
conditions the portfolio moves the median from 1.299 to 1.015, changes rho by more than
10% on 125, and crosses below 1 on 8 while never crossing back.

The reachable population is known in advance and it is small — after the portfolio, 23
matrices sit within 5% of the threshold and 40 within 20%. That caps the honest upside.
Early results: `impcol_a` 1.211 → 0.253, `oscil_dcop_06` 0.9997 → 0.9933. Full run in
progress.

## 4. Targeting rho directly: 22 predicted crossings, 2 verified solves

The search works as a search. Over 195 matrices it lowered the estimated rho(T_GS) on 154,
by 19.9% median, and pushed 22 from above 1 to below it — reaching further than the
"40 matrices within 20%" estimate suggested, with crossings from as high as rho = 3.6
(`orbitRaising_3`) and rho = 3.35 (`utm300`). It is cheap: 0.73 s median, 195 s for all 195.

Then the crossings were cashed in against the harness, because a predicted rho is not a
solved system ([`tools/verify_rho_search.py`](../tools/verify_rho_search.py)):

```
predicted crossings : 22
actually solved     :  2      (and one of those is rand_001_n5, a synthetic 5x5)
lost                :  0
```

Two reasons, and both matter more than the result.

1. **rho just under 1 is not convergence in any practical budget.** The crossings land at
   0.966, 0.976, 0.986, 0.991, 0.9996. Asymptotically guaranteed, unreachable at 1e-8
   inside the iteration cap. This is the same failure mode as the strictly diagonally
   dominant matrices in §5 below.
2. **The estimate itself is untrustworthy exactly where it matters.** 40 power iterations
   on a strongly non-normal operator cannot resolve rho to the third decimal, so some of
   these 22 never crossed at all. The estimator is honest about being a ranking device;
   using it as a threshold test asks more of it than it can give.

## 5. What all of this adds up to: permutation buys applicability, not convergence

Three independent attempts to improve *convergence* by permuting rows:

| approach | what it optimises | systems converted |
|---|---|---|
| bottleneck objective | the diagonal-dominance bound, exactly (365-0 vs MC64) | **+2** |
| rho search | the spectral radius, by direct descent (22 crossings) | **+2** |
| MC64 vs min-sum | the product of the diagonal | **0** (identical, 193/193) |

And yet the pipeline recovers 199 systems. Where does that come from? Decomposing every
gain by whether the method was *defined* beforehand:

```
                gained   diagonal empty before   diagonal full before
Jacobi             48            44  (92%)              4  (8%)
Gauss-Seidel       86            75  (87%)             11  (13%)
SOR                65            63  (97%)              2  (3%)
                  ---           ---                   ---
total             199           182  (91%)             17  (9%)
```

**91% of the gain is applicability**: a zero-free diagonal where there was none, which any
perfect matching supplies. Only 17 of 199 are matrices that already had a usable diagonal
and were made to converge.

> Row permutation buys applicability, not convergence. Three separate levers — an exactly
> solved dominance objective, a direct search on the spectral radius, and the established
> product objective — all converge on the same answer, and it is the same answer the
> decomposition gives independently.

That is the paper's real finding. It is negative, it has a mechanism, it is supported three
ways, and it tells anyone else in this area where not to spend their time. The
"+63% / +70% / +55%" headline stays true and stays measured; what changes is that its cause
is now known and stated.

### Why the near-1 regime dominates everything here

The same wall appears in the classical classes. Recomputed with the project's own rule —
denominators exclude matrices whose *system* is unsolvable (structurally singular, load
failed), which an earlier pass wrongly left in:

| class | solvable matrices | Jacobi | Gauss-Seidel | SOR |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| strict diagonal dominance | 43 | 88% | 88% | 88% |
| weak diagonal dominance | 46 | 83% | 83% | 85% |
| H-matrix | 83 | 77% | 81% | 76% |
| M-matrix | 34 | 65% | 76% | 76% |
| L-matrix | 38 | 58% | 71% | 68% |
| SPD | 102 | 27% | 51% | 55% |
| property A (proxy) | 117 | 24% | 29% | 28% |
| symmetric | 298 | 13% | 22% | 24% |
| **no theorem applies** | **679** | **0%** | **3%** | **3%** |

The theorems are never wrong: every strictly diagonally dominant matrix has rho(T_GS) < 1,
maximum 0.999999. The five SDD matrices that fail all sit at rho > 0.99 — guaranteed
convergence, no practical convergence. That gap between "converges" and "converges in
time" is what defeats every permutation lever in this project.

**Aggregate:** where some classical theorem applies (223 solvable matrices) Gauss-Seidel
solves 46%. Where none applies (679) it solves 3%.
